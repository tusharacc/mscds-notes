Q1 MIT §16.2 Exam

Answer each of the following independently.

  1. What is the probability that 0 does not appear among \(k\) digits chosen independently and uniformly at random from \(\{0,1,\ldots,9\}\)?
  2. A box contains 90 good and 10 defective screws. If we pick 10 screws at random (without replacement), what is the probability that none are defective?
  3. One digit is chosen uniformly at random from \(\{1,2,3,4,5\}\) and removed. A second digit is then chosen uniformly from the remaining four. What is the probability that the second digit picked is odd?
  4. You randomly permute \(1,2,\ldots,n\). What is the probability that digit \(k\) ends up in position \(i\)?
  5. A fair coin is flipped \(n\) times. What is the probability that all heads occur at the end of the sequence? (If no heads appear, this is vacuously true.)
Hint
(a) Each digit has probability 9/10 of not being 0, independently. (b) Count ways to choose 10 from the 90 good screws vs. all 100. (c) Use the Law of Total Probability conditioning on whether the first pick was odd or even. (d) All \(n!\) permutations are equally likely — what fraction send \(k\) to position \(i\)? (e) Count: how many of the \(2^n\) sequences have all heads at the end?
Solution
  1. Each of the \(k\) digits independently avoids 0 with probability \(9/10\). By independence: \[ P(\text{no 0}) = \left(\frac{9}{10}\right)^k \]
  2. We must choose all 10 from the 90 good screws: \[ P(\text{none defective}) = \frac{\binom{90}{10}}{\binom{100}{10}} = \frac{90 \cdot 89 \cdots 81}{100 \cdot 99 \cdots 91} \approx 0.3305 \]
  3. Condition on the first pick. \(P(\text{first odd}) = 3/5\), \(P(\text{first even}) = 2/5\). Given first was odd (removes one odd from \(\{1,3,5\}\)): 2 odd, 2 even remain, so \(P(\text{second odd} \mid \text{first odd}) = 2/4 = 1/2\). Given first was even: 3 odd, 1 even remain, so \(P(\text{second odd} \mid \text{first even}) = 3/4\). \[ P(\text{second odd}) = \frac{3}{5}\cdot\frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{5}\cdot\frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{10} + \frac{3}{10} = \frac{3}{5} \]
  4. All \(n!\) permutations are equally likely. Of these, \((n-1)!\) send digit \(k\) to position \(i\). Therefore: \[ P(k \text{ in position } i) = \frac{(n-1)!}{n!} = \frac{1}{n} \]
  5. A sequence has all heads at the end iff it looks like \(\underbrace{TT\cdots T}_{n-j}\underbrace{HH\cdots H}_{j}\) for some \(0 \le j \le n\). There are exactly \(n+1\) such sequences out of \(2^n\) total, so: \[ P(\text{all heads at end}) = \frac{n+1}{2^n} \]
✓ High — MIT 6.042J §16, standard exam problems with published solutions.
Q2 MIT §16.3 Class

The New York Yankees and the Boston Red Sox play a two-out-of-three series: they play until one team wins 2 games, then the series ends. Assume the Red Sox win each game with probability \(3/5\), independently.

  1. What is the probability that exactly 3 games are played?
  2. What is the probability that the winner of the series lost the first game?
  3. What is the probability that the Red Sox win the series?
Hint
Draw the full game tree. The series lasts 3 games iff the first two games split. For (b), find the probability that a team wins the series after losing game 1 — which requires winning both games 2 and 3. For (c), sum over all sequences where Red Sox win exactly 2 games first.
Solution

Let \(p = 3/5\) (Red Sox win), \(q = 2/5\) (Yankees win).

  1. 3 games are played iff games 1 and 2 split (one win each): \[ P(\text{3 games}) = pq + qp = 2pq = 2 \cdot \frac{3}{5} \cdot \frac{2}{5} = \frac{12}{25} \]
  2. The winner lost game 1 iff: (NYY wins g1 \(\cap\) RS wins g2 \(\cap\) RS wins g3) or (RS wins g1 \(\cap\) NYY wins g2 \(\cap\) NYY wins g3): \[ P = qp^2 + pq^2 = pq(p+q) = pq = \frac{6}{25} \]
  3. Red Sox can win in 2 or 3 games: \begin{align} P(\text{RS wins in 2}) &= p^2 = \tfrac{9}{25}\\ P(\text{RS wins in 3}) &= qp^2 + pqp = 2pq \cdot p = 2 \cdot \tfrac{6}{25} \cdot \tfrac{3}{5} = \tfrac{36}{125}\\ P(\text{RS wins series}) &= \tfrac{9}{25} + \tfrac{36}{125} = \tfrac{45}{125}+\tfrac{36}{125} = \boxed{\tfrac{81}{125}} \end{align}

Q3 MIT §16.4 Class

To decide who gets a prize, a coin with \(P(H) = p\) is flipped repeatedly in pairs. If the pair is HT, Player 1 wins. If TH, Player 2 wins. If HH or TT, the pair is discarded and two more flips are made.

  1. Use the four-step method to find a simple formula for the probability that Player 1 wins.
  2. What is the probability that neither player ever wins (the game goes on forever)?
Hint
Condition on each "round" (pair of flips). Let \(W\) be the event Player 1 wins. In one round: HT has probability \(p(1-p)\), TH has probability \((1-p)p\). What fraction of decisive rounds go to Player 1?
Solution

In each independent round:

  • \(P(\text{HT}) = p(1-p)\) → Player 1 wins
  • \(P(\text{TH}) = (1-p)p\) → Player 2 wins
  • \(P(\text{HH or TT}) = p^2 + (1-p)^2\) → redo
  1. Since HT and TH are equally likely (both equal \(p(1-p)\)), Player 1 wins exactly half of all decisive rounds: \[ P(\text{Player 1 wins}) = \frac{p(1-p)}{p(1-p)+p(1-p)} = \frac{1}{2} \] This holds for any \(p \in (0,1)\) — the game is always fair!
  2. The game goes on forever only if every round is indecisive (HH or TT). The probability of this over infinitely many rounds is: \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} [p^2+(1-p)^2]^n = 0 \quad \text{for } 0 < p < 1 \] So \(P(\text{neither wins}) = 0\). (Exception: \(p=0\) or \(p=1\), where the game never terminates.)
✓ High — classic symmetry argument, well-known result.
Q4 MIT §16.5 Homework

A variant of Let's Make a Deal with four doors. A prize is hidden behind one door uniformly at random. The contestant picks a door. The host then opens one unpicked door with no prize. The contestant may stick or switch to one of the two remaining unpicked doors.

  1. Contestant Stu always stays. What is his probability of winning?
  2. Contestant Zelda always switches, choosing uniformly among the two remaining doors. What is her probability of winning?
  3. Carol always opens the earliest (alphabetically) available non-prize, non-picked door. Mergatroid always switches to the earliest remaining non-initial, non-opened door. What is Mergatroid's probability of winning?
Hint
For (a) and (b), no case analysis needed — use the law of total probability conditioning on whether the initial pick is correct. For (c), assume Mergatroid picks door 1 (WLOG by symmetry) and enumerate over the 4 possible prize locations.
Solution
  1. Stu's initial pick is correct with probability \(1/4\). Staying never changes this: \(P(\text{Stu wins}) = \mathbf{1/4}\).
  2. Two cases: (i) initial pick correct (prob \(1/4\)): switching to either of 2 remaining doors misses the prize → wins with prob 0. (ii) initial pick wrong (prob \(3/4\)): 1 prize among 2 remaining doors; Zelda picks one uniformly → wins with prob \(1/2\). \[ P(\text{Zelda wins}) = \tfrac{1}{4}\cdot 0 + \tfrac{3}{4}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} = \mathbf{3/8} \]
  3. Assume Mergatroid picks door 1. Carol opens the earliest non-prize, non-picked door. Mergatroid then picks the earliest remaining unchosen door.
    • Prize at door 1: Carol opens door 2. Remaining: \{3,4\}. Mergatroid picks 3. Loses.
    • Prize at door 2: Carol opens door 3 (earliest non-prize, non-picked). Remaining: \{2,4\}. Mergatroid picks 2. Wins.
    • Prize at door 3: Carol opens door 2 (earliest available). Remaining: \{3,4\}. Mergatroid picks 3. Wins.
    • Prize at door 4: Carol opens door 2. Remaining: \{3,4\}. Mergatroid picks 3. Loses.
    \[ P(\text{Mergatroid wins}) = \tfrac{1}{4}(0+1+1+0) = \mathbf{1/2} \]
✓ High — MIT 6.042J homework problem, case-by-case enumeration is decisive.
Q5 MIT §16.6 Homework

There are \(n\) Immortal Warriors. They forge a coin with \(P(H) = p\). Each Immortal flips the coin once. If exactly one flips heads, that Immortal is declared "The One." Otherwise the protocol fails.

  1. Write a formula for the probability of success as a function of \(p\) and \(n\).
  2. What value of \(p\) maximises the probability of success?
  3. What is the maximum probability of success? What does it approach as \(n \to \infty\)?
Hint
Success means exactly one head in \(n\) independent Bernoulli(\(p\)) flips — this is a binomial probability. To maximise, differentiate with respect to \(p\) and set to zero.
Solution
  1. Success is the event \(X = 1\) where \(X \sim \text{Bin}(n, p)\): \[ P(\text{success}) = \binom{n}{1} p^1 (1-p)^{n-1} = np(1-p)^{n-1} \]
  2. Differentiate with respect to \(p\) and set to zero: \[ \frac{d}{dp}\bigl[np(1-p)^{n-1}\bigr] = n\bigl[(1-p)^{n-1} - p(n-1)(1-p)^{n-2}\bigr] = n(1-p)^{n-2}\bigl[(1-p) - p(n-1)\bigr] = 0 \] This gives \(1-p = p(n-1)\), so \(\boxed{p = 1/n}\).
  3. Substituting \(p = 1/n\): \[ P_{\max} = n \cdot \frac{1}{n} \cdot \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)^{n-1} = \left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{n-1} \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} e^{-1} \approx 0.368 \] So even with the optimal coin, the success probability is bounded above by \(1/e\).
✓ High — standard optimisation over binomial, limit is the classic \((1-1/n)^n \to e^{-1}\) result.
Q6 MIT §16.8 Class

A system has \(n\) components. Each component independently fails within one year with probability \(p\). The system fails if any component fails. Let \(F\) be the event that the system fails.

  1. Show that \(P(F) \ge p\).
  2. Show that \(P(F) \le np\).
  3. Show that both bounds are tight (achievable).
Hint
For the lower bound, use monotonicity: \(F \supseteq F_1\). For the upper bound, use the union bound. For tightness, consider perfectly correlated failures (all fail together) and mutually exclusive failures.
Solution

Let \(F_i\) be the event that component \(i\) fails. Then \(F = F_1 \cup F_2 \cup \cdots \cup F_n\) and \(P(F_i) = p\) for all \(i\).

  1. Since \(F_1 \subseteq F\), monotonicity of probability gives \(P(F) \ge P(F_1) = p\). \(\square\)
  2. By the Union Bound: \(P(F) = P\!\bigl(\bigcup F_i\bigr) \le \sum_{i=1}^n P(F_i) = np\). \(\square\)
  3. Lower bound is tight: Make all \(F_i\) identical (perfectly correlated — they all depend on the same coin flip). Then \(P(F) = P(F_1) = p\). \(\checkmark\) Upper bound is tight: Make the \(F_i\) mutually exclusive, each with probability \(p\) (possible if \(np \le 1\)). Then \(P(F) = \sum P(F_i) = np\). \(\checkmark\)
✓ High — direct application of monotonicity and union bound; both achievability constructions are explicit.

Q7 MIT §17.2 Practice

Dirty Harry places two bullets randomly into 2 of the 6 chambers of his revolver, then gives the cylinder a random spin and holds the gun to your heart.

  1. What is the probability you get shot if he pulls the trigger once?
  2. He pulls the trigger once and you survive. Without re-spinning, he pulls again. What is the probability you get shot?
  3. Suppose you noticed that Harry placed the two bullets in adjacent chambers. How does this change the answers to (a) and (b)?
Hint
For (a): how many of the 6 chambers have bullets? For (b): given the first chamber was empty, how many of the 5 remaining chambers have bullets? For (c): with adjacent bullets, enumerate the 6 equally likely starting positions and count.
Solution
  1. 2 of 6 chambers contain bullets; random spin makes each equally likely: \(P(\text{shot}) = 2/6 = \mathbf{1/3}\).
  2. Given the first pull was empty: one of the 5 remaining chambers was active; 2 bullets are still in those 5: \[ P(\text{shot on 2nd} \mid \text{alive after 1st}) = \frac{2}{5} \]
  3. With adjacent bullets, think of the cylinder as a circle of 6 positions. There are 6 equally likely starting positions (where the cylinder points). The two bullets occupy 2 consecutive chambers.
    • (a) unchanged: 2 of 6 positions are bullets, so \(P(\text{shot on 1st}) = \mathbf{1/3}\).
    • (b) changed: of the 4 starting positions where you survive the first pull, only 1 of them has a bullet immediately next (the position just before the first bullet). The other 3 have an empty chamber next. \[ P(\text{shot on 2nd} \mid \text{alive after 1st, adjacent bullets}) = \frac{1}{4} \] (Lower than \(2/5\), so knowing the bullets are adjacent is good news.)
✓ High — classic conditional probability problem; parts (a)/(b) are elementary, part (c) via direct enumeration.
Q8 MIT §17.5 Class

There are two decks of cards: one is a complete 52-card deck, the other is missing the Ace of Spades (51 cards). You pick one deck with equal probability and draw one card uniformly at random from it. You observe that the drawn card is the 8 of Hearts. What is the probability that you picked the complete deck?

Hint
Apply Bayes' theorem. Both decks contain the 8♥, but with different probabilities: \(1/52\) vs. \(1/51\). Which deck makes the 8♥ more likely?
Solution

Let \(C\) = complete deck, \(I\) = incomplete deck (missing A♠). Both contain 8♥.

\[ P(8\heartsuit \mid C) = \frac{1}{52}, \quad P(8\heartsuit \mid I) = \frac{1}{51}, \quad P(C) = P(I) = \frac{1}{2} \]

By Bayes' theorem:

\[ P(C \mid 8\heartsuit) = \frac{P(8\heartsuit \mid C)\,P(C)}{P(8\heartsuit \mid C)\,P(C) + P(8\heartsuit \mid I)\,P(I)} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{52}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}}{\tfrac{1}{52}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{51}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{52}}{\tfrac{1}{52}+\tfrac{1}{51}} = \frac{51}{103} \]

Intuition: drawing the 8♥ is slightly less likely from the complete deck (1/52) than the incomplete deck (1/51), so we update downward from the prior \(1/2\): \(51/103 < 1/2\).

✓ High — direct Bayes calculation; result 51/103 is exact.
Q9 MIT §17.7 Class

Three prisoners — Sauron (S), Voldemort (V), and Foo-Foo (F) — are held. The parole board will release exactly two of them, chosen uniformly at random. A guard offers to tell Sauron the name of one other prisoner who will be released, choosing uniformly at random if both others qualify. Sauron figures his release probability is \(2/3\) before asking. The guard says: "Foo-Foo will be released."

What is the probability that Sauron is released, given the guard's statement? Has his probability changed?

Hint
There are 3 equally likely release pairs: \(\{S,V\}\), \(\{S,F\}\), \(\{V,F\}\). Model the guard's strategy explicitly: he names one releasee other than Sauron, choosing uniformly if both qualify. Compute \(P(\text{guard says F})\) via total probability.
Solution

The three release pairs are equally likely with probability \(1/3\) each.

The guard names one of \(\{V, F\}\) who is being released (never names Sauron):

  • \(\{S,V\}\) released: guard must say "V" (only option). \(P(\text{says F} \mid \{S,V\}) = 0\).
  • \(\{S,F\}\) released: guard must say "F". \(P(\text{says F} \mid \{S,F\}) = 1\).
  • \(\{V,F\}\) released: guard chooses uniformly. \(P(\text{says F} \mid \{V,F\}) = 1/2\).
\[ P(\text{says F}) = 0\cdot\tfrac{1}{3} + 1\cdot\tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{2} \] \[ P(S\text{ released} \mid \text{says F}) = \frac{P(\text{says F} \mid \{S,F\})\cdot P(\{S,F\})}{P(\text{says F})} = \frac{1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3}}{\tfrac{1}{2}} = \frac{2}{3} \]

Sauron's probability has not changed — it remains \(2/3\). The guard's statement gives him no new information about his own fate. This is unlike the Monty Hall problem because here the guard is equally likely to name either releasee when both are eligible.

✓ High — this is the "Sleeping Beauty"-adjacent prisoner problem; the Bayesian calculation is exact.
Q10 MIT §17.8 Homework

Every Skywalker serves either the light side or the dark side. The first Skywalker serves the dark side. For \(n \ge 2\), the \(n\)-th Skywalker serves the same side as the \((n-1)\)-th with probability \(1/4\), and the opposite side with probability \(3/4\). Let \(d_n = P(n\text{-th Skywalker serves dark})\).

  1. Write a recurrence for \(d_n\) with base case.
  2. Find a closed-form formula for \(d_n\).
Hint
For the recurrence: condition on whether the \((n-1)\)-th served dark. The recurrence will be linear of the form \(d_n = a \cdot d_{n-1} + b\). Find the fixed point \(d^*\), subtract it, and solve the resulting geometric sequence.
Solution
  1. By the law of total probability conditioning on the \((n-1)\)-th Skywalker: \[ d_n = d_{n-1}\cdot\frac{1}{4} + (1-d_{n-1})\cdot\frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{4} - \frac{1}{2}\,d_{n-1}, \quad d_1 = 1 \]
  2. Fixed point: \(d^* = 3/4 - d^*/2 \Rightarrow d^* = 1/2\). Let \(e_n = d_n - 1/2\): \[ e_n = -\tfrac{1}{2}\,e_{n-1}, \quad e_1 = 1/2 \] So \(e_n = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot(-\tfrac{1}{2})^{n-1} = \tfrac{(-1)^{n-1}}{2^n}\), giving: \[ \boxed{d_n = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{2^n}} \] Check: \(d_1 = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1\) ✓; \(d_2 = 1/2 - 1/4 = 1/4\) and \(3/4 - 1/2 = 1/4\) ✓. As \(n \to \infty\), \(d_n \to 1/2\).
✓ High — linear recurrence solved by standard fixed-point substitution.
Q11 MIT §17.10 Homework

Oscar tries to levitate with probability \(1/6\), and Liz tries to become a world-champion flaming torch juggler with probability \(1/4\). These events are independent.

  1. What is the probability that both Oscar and Liz succeed?
  2. What is the probability that Oscar succeeds but Liz fails?
  3. What is the probability that at least one of them succeeds?
Hint
Independence means \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\cdot P(B)\). For (c) use complementary counting: \(P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{neither})\).
Solution

Let \(O\) = Oscar succeeds, \(L\) = Liz succeeds. \(P(O) = 1/6\), \(P(L) = 1/4\), independent.

  1. \(P(O \cap L) = P(O)\cdot P(L) = \tfrac{1}{6}\cdot\tfrac{1}{4} = \mathbf{1/24}\)
  2. \(P(O \cap L^c) = P(O)\cdot P(L^c) = \tfrac{1}{6}\cdot\tfrac{3}{4} = \mathbf{1/8}\)
  3. \(P(O \cup L) = 1 - P(O^c \cap L^c) = 1 - \tfrac{5}{6}\cdot\tfrac{3}{4} = 1 - \tfrac{5}{8} = \mathbf{3/8}\)
✓ High — straightforward application of independence.
Q12 MIT §17.11 Homework

In a subject, 10% of assigned problems contain errors. If you ask a Teaching Assistant whether a problem has an error, the TA gives the correct answer 95% of the time (independently). You ask the TA about a problem and the TA says it has an error. What is the probability the problem actually contains an error?

Hint
Let \(E\) = problem has error, \(T\) = TA says error. You want \(P(E \mid T)\). Compute \(P(T)\) via total probability, then apply Bayes.
Solution

\(P(E) = 0.1\). \(P(T \mid E) = 0.95\). \(P(T \mid E^c) = 0.05\) (TA wrong on error-free problems).

\[ P(T) = P(T\mid E)P(E) + P(T\mid E^c)P(E^c) = 0.95\cdot 0.10 + 0.05\cdot 0.90 = 0.095 + 0.045 = 0.14 \] \[ P(E \mid T) = \frac{P(T\mid E)\,P(E)}{P(T)} = \frac{0.095}{0.14} = \frac{19}{28} \approx 67.9\% \]

Despite the TA's 95% accuracy, the base rate of 10% errors means that only about 68% of "TA says error" reports are genuine — a concrete illustration of the base-rate fallacy.

✓ High — standard Bayes with explicit numbers; result 19/28 is exact.
Q13 MIT §17.13 Homework

A 52-card deck is shuffled and you are dealt a hand of 13 cards. Use conditional probability to find the probability that your hand contains at least one Ace.

Hint
Use the complement: \(P(\ge 1 \text{ Ace}) = 1 - P(\text{no Aces})\). Count the 13-card hands with no Aces using \(\binom{48}{13}\) (choosing all from the 48 non-Ace cards).
Solution
\[ P(\text{at least 1 Ace}) = 1 - P(\text{no Aces}) = 1 - \frac{\binom{48}{13}}{\binom{52}{13}} \]

Simplifying the ratio (all 13 cards chosen from 48 non-Aces vs. all 52):

\[ \frac{\binom{48}{13}}{\binom{52}{13}} = \frac{48!\,/\,(13!\cdot 35!)}{52!\,/\,(13!\cdot 39!)} = \frac{48!\cdot 39!}{52!\cdot 35!} = \frac{39\cdot 38\cdot 37\cdot 36}{52\cdot 51\cdot 50\cdot 49} \]

Numerically: \(39\cdot38\cdot37\cdot36 = 1{,}974{,}024\) and \(52\cdot51\cdot50\cdot49 = 6{,}497{,}400\), so:

\[ P(\ge 1\text{ Ace}) = 1 - \frac{1{,}974{,}024}{6{,}497{,}400} \approx 1 - 0.3037 \approx \mathbf{0.696} \]

Q14 MIT §17.16 Exam

There are two decks. The red deck has 26 red and 26 black cards. The blue deck has 30 red and 22 black cards. You pick a deck with equal probability and draw one card uniformly at random. The card is red. What is the probability you drew from the red deck?

Hint
Apply Bayes' theorem: \(P(\text{red deck} \mid \text{red card}) \propto P(\text{red card} \mid \text{red deck})\cdot P(\text{red deck})\). Which deck makes a red card more likely?
Solution
\[ P(\text{red}\mid\text{red deck}) = \frac{26}{52} = \frac{1}{2}, \quad P(\text{red}\mid\text{blue deck}) = \frac{30}{52} = \frac{15}{26} \] \begin{align} P(\text{red deck}\mid\text{red card}) &= \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}}{\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{15}{26}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{4}}{\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{15}{52}} = \frac{\tfrac{13}{52}}{\tfrac{13}{52}+\tfrac{15}{52}} = \frac{13}{28} \end{align}

Since the blue deck has more red cards, observing a red card shifts probability toward the blue deck. The posterior \(13/28 < 1/2\) confirms this.

✓ High — direct Bayes; fractions simplify cleanly to 13/28.
Q15 MIT §17.18 Exam

A rare disease occurs in 0.1% of the population (\(P(D) = 0.001\)). A diagnostic test has sensitivity \(P(\text{+}\mid D) = 0.99\) and specificity \(P(\text{–}\mid D^c) = 0.99\). A randomly selected person tests positive. What is the probability they actually have the disease?

Hint
False positive rate \(= P(\text{+}\mid D^c) = 1 - \text{specificity} = 0.01\). The disease is rare, so there are many more healthy people than sick — even with 99% specificity, most positive tests are false positives.
Solution
\[ P(+\mid D^c) = 1 - 0.99 = 0.01 \] \begin{align} P(+) &= P(+\mid D)P(D) + P(+\mid D^c)P(D^c)\\ &= 0.99\times 0.001 + 0.01\times 0.999 = 0.00099 + 0.00999 = 0.01098 \end{align} \[ P(D\mid +) = \frac{0.99\times 0.001}{0.01098} = \frac{0.00099}{0.01098} \approx \mathbf{9.0\%} \]

A positive result raises the probability from 0.1% to only ~9%, because for every 1 true positive there are roughly 10 false positives (1000 healthy people × 0.01 false positive rate = 10 false positives per 1 true positive). This is the base-rate fallacy.

✓ High — standard medical testing example; the ~9% result is well-documented.
Q16 MIT §17.19 Exam

In the classic Monty Hall problem: prize is equally likely behind door 1, 2, or 3. You pick door 1. Solution

Hint: Let \(\Omega = \{\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_n\}\) be the sample space. Set \(\mathbf{p} = (\sqrt{p_1},\ldots,\sqrt{p_n})\) and \(\mathbf{r} = (|R(\omega_1)-\mu|\sqrt{p_1},\ldots,|R(\omega_n)-\mu|\sqrt{p_n})\). Compute \(|\mathbf{p}|\), \(|\mathbf{r}|\), and \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{p}\).

Hint
Once you verify \(|\mathbf{p}|=1\), \(|\mathbf{r}|=\sigma\), and \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{p} = E[|R-\mu|]\), apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality \(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{p} \le |\mathbf{r}|\,|\mathbf{p}|\).
Solution

Proof: Define real vectors \(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{r} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) as in the hint.

\[ |\mathbf{p}|^2 = \sum_i (\sqrt{p_i})^2 = \sum_i p_i = 1 \implies |\mathbf{p}| = 1 \] \[ |\mathbf{r}|^2 = \sum_i (|R(\omega_i)-\mu|)^2 p_i = E[(R-\mu)^2] = \sigma^2 \implies |\mathbf{r}| = \sigma \] \[ \mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{p} = \sum_i |R(\omega_i)-\mu|\sqrt{p_i}\cdot\sqrt{p_i} = \sum_i |R(\omega_i)-\mu|\,p_i = E[|R-\mu|] \]

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\[ E[|R-\mu|] = \mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{p} \le |\mathbf{r}|\,|\mathbf{p}| = \sigma \cdot 1 = \sigma \quad\square \]

Equality holds when \(\mathbf{r}\) and \(\mathbf{p}\) are parallel, i.e., when \(|R(\omega_i)-\mu|\) is constant — a two-point distribution like \(\pm\sigma\) with equal probability.

✓ High — the vector/Cauchy-Schwarz construction from the MIT hint is the intended proof; result is tight for symmetric two-point distributions.

A herd of cows is struck by cold-cow disease. The disease lowered the average herd temperature to 85°F; the lowest observed temperature was 70°F. A cow dies if its temperature falls below 90°F.

  1. Use Markov's inequality to prove that at most 3/4 of the cows survive.
  2. With 400 cows, construct an explicit example achieving this bound.
  3. Justify why Markov's inequality applies. What random variable should you use?
Hint
You cannot apply Markov directly to temperature (temperatures might be negative). Instead, shift: define \(V = T - 70 \ge 0\) and apply Markov to \(V\). Survival means \(T \ge 90\), i.e., \(V \ge 20\).
Solution
  1. Let \(T\) = temperature of a randomly chosen cow. Define \(V = T - 70 \ge 0\) (since minimum is 70°F). \[ E[V] = E[T] - 70 = 85 - 70 = 15 \] A cow survives iff \(T \ge 90\), i.e., \(V \ge 20\).   2. With 400 cows: put 300 at exactly 90°F (survive) and 100 at exactly 70°F (die). Mean: \[ \frac{300\times 90 + 100\times 70}{400} = \frac{27000 + 7000}{400} = 85\,^\circ\text{F} \checkmark \] Survival fraction = 300/400 = 3/4. Bound is tight.
  3. Sample space: the 400 cows, each equally likely to be chosen. \(T\) is a random variable mapping cows to their temperature. \(E[T]\) is the herd's average temperature = 85°F. Markov applies because \(V = T-70 \ge 0\) is a non-negative random variable with known expectation.
